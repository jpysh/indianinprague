What Europe's search for alternative healing can teach us

Kavyanjali · 25 August 2026 · 6 min read

Yoga studios on every second corner, incense in boho shops, pranayama before work. Europe is not importing an aesthetic, it is recovering a way of living, and that is why these traditions land the way they do.

On this page

  1. We are exhausted and need another way to live
  2. Personal growth does not always mean becoming more
  3. Spiritual balance also means planetary balance
  4. Building a bridge, not a shop

There is a glimmer of joy I feel whenever I see a man wearing a Rudraksh beads mala, or an "Om" tattoo on a woman, here on the streets of Prague.

When I moved here, I expected the usual polite curiosity about India: about our food, the places to visit, the weddings. What I did not expect was how many people would want to talk to me about our lifestyle. Ayurveda, yoga, spirituality, and Hindu and Buddhist philosophy. There are yoga studios on every second corner, incense sticks are sold from rustic and boho shops, and people practise pranayama in the morning.

But none of it is really about India. It is about three things:

  • Exhaustion. Mental and physical, and the growing sense that there must be another way to live.
  • Personal growth that looks Eastward, and asks what to remove rather than what to add.
  • Planetary balance, reached through eco-friendly, non-extractive, centuries-old practices that were sidetracked on our march towards industrial and technological development.

1We are exhausted and need another way to live

Modern life has given humanity extraordinary things. But somewhere along the way, we became very good at developing the world around us while becoming less certain about how to care for the world within us.

The reason Ayurveda, yoga, and Hindu and other Eastern philosophies land so well in the middle of a mental health crisis is that the burnout has structural causes. Europe has built an extraordinary standard of living on a work culture that leaves very little room for the nervous system, and people are noticing.

Western medicine, brilliant as it is, tends to treat the mind as an organ that goes wrong. Ayurveda never separated the mind from the body, the body from the season, the season from what you eat, or any of it from how you live.

There is science behind these spiritual practices. But it is behavioural science mixed with a better poetry of life and earth. Oil on the scalp, warm food, sleeping and rising with the light, spices in food, silence in the first hour of the day. A psychologist would call these sleep hygiene, circadian regulation, and somatic self-soothing.

My mother calls it daily routine.

Yoga asanas are a preparation. A way of making the body quiet and flexible enough that the mind becomes bearable to sit inside.

2Personal growth does not always mean becoming more

Modern culture often teaches us that personal growth means adding. More knowledge, qualifications, wealth, experiences, achievements, followers. The list is endless. Ancient Eastern philosophies can offer a rather different question: what if growth sometimes means removing?

  • Unnecessary noise and constant stimulation.
  • The need to prove our existence.
  • Habits that keep us disconnected from our bodies, from other people, and from nature.

Yoga, meditation, Ayurveda and Buddhist traditions are enormously diverse and cannot simply be placed into one category called "Eastern wisdom". Yet many of them share an interest in awareness, balance, discipline, compassion, and the relationship between our inner life and the world around us.

That is perhaps why they resonate so strongly today. They invite us to become more conscious of the person we already are, rather than promising a better version of that person. And that can be a very different kind of personal growth.

3Spiritual balance also means planetary balance

Wellbeing cannot ultimately be separated from the wellbeing of the planet. We can meditate in beautiful rooms, practise yoga every morning and fill our homes with calming Ayurvedic foods and fragrances. But if the way we live depends upon endless extraction, endless consumption and endless waste, something remains out of balance.

Many traditional societies understood something that industrial civilisation has often struggled to remember.

We are not separate from nature. We are nature.

Across India and elsewhere, generations developed ways of living with plants, natural fibres, herbs, oils, wood and clay that were closely connected to local environments and seasonal rhythms. None of this asks the earth for very much:

  • A copper vessel that lasts three generations.
  • Sesame oil pressed from a seed.
  • Textile woven on a natural handloom, strong enough to be passed from mothers to daughters.
  • Neem, turmeric and tulsi, grown in backyards rather than factories.

These practices were never designed to be extracted, scaled or discarded, because they came from a way of thinking in which the self, the household, the field and the season were one continuous system.

We set that thinking aside for two centuries, believing it was the slow road. It turns out it was simply the road that could be walked forever. Of course, we should not romanticise the past: ancient ways of living were not automatically sustainable, perfect or universally applicable. But there is wisdom worth revisiting.

Building a bridge, not a shop

I sell Ayurvedic products, handloom meditation clothing, and the small objects that make a practice possible. I started this work because I watched people here in Europe reaching towards traditions that my family, my culture and generations before me had held with respect for a very long time. Reaching for a slower relationship with the body, the mind and the world around them.

And often, what they found first was the loudest and cheapest version of it. A mass-produced object stripped of its story of origin and place. Something presented as exotic, when it could have been presented simply as human.

I want to offer things with a little more care behind them, and not turn Indian culture into a costume or a marketing aesthetic. Things made by people, rather than designed to look handmade. Natural materials where they genuinely make sense. Handloom traditions seen as living crafts rather than decorative trends. Ayurvedic products approached with respect for the tradition behind them.

Perhaps what I am really creating is more than a shop. It is a small bridge between worlds. Between an India that I know personally and a Europe that is asking new questions. Between old knowledge and modern life. Between consumption and conscious choice.

If someone buys a meditation shawl from me and it simply makes them feel comfortable while sitting quietly, that is already enough. If an Ayurvedic product encourages them to think differently about their daily routine, wonderful.

Hopefully the object becomes an invitation: to slow down, learn, notice, practise, and perhaps eventually to need fewer things rather than more.

The best part is that Europe is already reaching towards these old traditions in its search for a healthier, calmer and more conscious way of living. It is not adopting Eastern practice as an aesthetic import. It is leading the way in recovering a technology of living that our own march towards progress mislaid.

And I am so excited to be part of this story.
